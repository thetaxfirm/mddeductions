## How Section 1245 Recapture Works on Medical Equipment

Medical equipment is §1245 property. Every MACRS deduction and every §168(k) bonus depreciation dollar you claim reduces your adjusted basis in the asset. When you sell, the IRS recaptures the spread between sale price and adjusted basis, not as a capital gain. It recaptures it as ordinary income, dollar for dollar, up to the total depreciation taken.

That distinction matters more than most operators realize. Capital gains treatment only applies to appreciation *above* your original cost basis, if any exists. The depreciation you took, all of it, comes back as ordinary income first.

### Ordinary Income vs. Capital Gain: Where Recapture Sits

Say you paid $500,000 for a therapy unit, depreciated it to zero, then sold it for $200,000. The full $200,000 is §1245 recapture income, taxed as ordinary income. There is no long-term capital gain in that transaction, because the sale price never exceeded your original cost.

If you sold it for $550,000, the first $500,000 of gain is ordinary income (recapture), and only the $50,000 above original cost qualifies as long-term capital gain, assuming you've held the asset over one year.

### Bonus Depreciation and the Recapture Acceleration Problem

Under §168(k), an operator who places a $400,000 Plasmapheresis system into service in year one and claims 100% bonus depreciation has a $400,000 recapture exposure from that same day. The holding period clock starts running, but it does not reduce the recapture figure. The basis is zero. Any sale proceeds, regardless of when they occur, are fully recapturable.

This is the core tension of front-loaded depreciation: the deduction is immediate, but so is the recapture liability. For more on [how §168(k) bonus depreciation works on medical equipment](mddeductions.com/blog/active-income-medical-equipment-business-168k-bonus-depreciation) before taking that deduction, the mechanics matter at both ends of the cycle. The full qualification framework is in the [§168(k) deduction rules for medical devices](mddeductions.com/blog/section-168k-bonus-depreciation-medical-equipment).

## Section 1245 Property Recapture Rate: What You Actually Owe

The section 1245 property recapture rate is the federal ordinary income rate, up to 37% in 2026. The maximum long-term capital gains rate is 20%. That's a spread of up to 17 percentage points on the same sale proceeds, which on a $500,000 equipment sale is up to $85,000 in additional federal tax versus capital gains treatment.

### Federal Ordinary Income Rates vs. Long-Term Capital Gains Rates

For a high-income operator, a physician, surgeon, or business owner in the top bracket, §1245 recapture is taxed at 37% federally. State income tax layers on top. California adds another 13.3% on ordinary income; states with no income tax add zero. In high-tax states, the combined marginal rate on recapture income can exceed 50%.

Long-term capital gains rates cap at 20% federally, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax for high earners, bringing the federal ceiling to 23.8%. Still materially lower than the ordinary income ceiling. The recapture rate differential is the primary reason exit planning deserves as much attention as the initial deduction.

## Holding Period Strategy and the Equipment Sale Depreciation Clawback

This is where most operators hold a misconception. Holding medical equipment beyond one year does not reduce or eliminate §1245 recapture. The medical equipment sale depreciation clawback is fully ordinary income regardless of how long you owned the asset.

### Why the One-Year Mark Does Not Eliminate Recapture

The one-year threshold governs *character* of gain on appreciation above basis. It has no effect on §1245 recapture, which is determined entirely by how much depreciation you claimed, not how long you held the asset. An operator who holds a bonus-depreciated unit for five years and sells it below original cost still recognizes 100% of the recapture as ordinary income.

The holding period matters in one narrow scenario: if the sale price exceeds original cost, the excess qualifies as long-term capital gain after one year. But for equipment that's been fully depreciated and sells at or below its original price, the common case, the holding period is irrelevant to the tax character.

### Optimal Holding Windows for Medical Equipment Operations

A multi-year operating horizon does reduce the *effective* cost of recapture in one important way: it spreads the income offset across years. If a $400,000 deduction saves you $148,000 in year one (at 37%), and you generate $400,000 of operating income across years one through four that the deduction sheltered, the eventual recapture hit is paid from a position of realized operational benefit. The longer the asset earns revenue before disposition, the better the net economics, even though the recapture tax itself doesn't shrink.

## Exit Timing and Pricing: Minimizing Recapture Tax When You Sell

Operators have fewer tools for deferring recapture than they often expect. Two commonly assumed strategies, installment sales and §1031 exchanges, have significant limitations when applied to medical equipment.

### Installment Sales as a Recapture Management Tool

An installment sale under §453 spreads principal payments across years, which can defer capital gains tax. It does not defer §1245 recapture. Under §453(i), recapture income is recognized in full in the year of sale, regardless of when payments are received. The recapture tax is due in year one of the installment arrangement, even if you haven't yet collected the cash.

This catches operators off guard. Installment sales can still make sense for managing the gain *above* recapture, but they are not a recapture deferral tool.

### Equipment Trade-In and Like-Kind Exchange Limitations

Post-TCJA, §1031 like-kind exchanges apply only to real property. Personal property, including all medical devices, was explicitly removed from §1031 eligibility by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017. An equipment swap or trade-in does not defer recapture under any exchange structure currently available.

Trade-ins can reduce *recognized gain* by offsetting the sale price against the new equipment's list value in a dealer transaction, but that's a pricing negotiation tool, not a tax deferral mechanism. The recapture on the surrendered equipment is still triggered.

## Replacement Equipment Planning and the Recapture Tax on Bonus Depreciation

The most effective tool for managing recapture tax on bonus depreciation is also the most operationally natural one: buy replacement equipment in the same tax year you sell the old unit.

### Stacking a New Deduction Against the Recapture in the Same Year

If an operator sells an EBOO therapy unit generating $300,000 of recapture income and simultaneously places a new $350,000 unit into service under §168(k), the new deduction can largely neutralize the recapture hit within the same filing year, provided the income is active and the operator materially participates in the business.

The [EBOO therapy equipment deduction mechanics](mddeductions.com/blog/eboo-therapy-equipment-business-deduction) and [Plasmapheresis equipment depreciation and tax structure](mddeductions.com/blog/plasmapheresis-business-tax-deduction-guide) each carry their own cost bases and depreciation schedules. When a replacement is strategically timed, the §168(k) deduction on the new asset offsets the §1245 recapture income from the old one at the same marginal rate, effectively zeroing out the net taxable event if the numbers align.

When evaluating the replacement purchase, the choice between [§179 versus bonus depreciation for medical devices](mddeductions.com/blog/section-179-medical-device-depreciation-limit-2024) affects how the deduction is structured, particularly if you have passive income constraints or state conformity issues. The key variable is ensuring the new deduction lands in the same tax year as the recapture recognition.

Material participation is a prerequisite. Passive activity rules under §469 can prevent a new §168(k) deduction from offsetting active recapture income if the operator doesn't meet the activity thresholds. The [IRS operating business requirements for medical devices](mddeductions.com/blog/medical-device-operating-business-requirements-irs) governs whether the offset strategy is available to a given operator.

## Multi-Year Tax Forecast: Planning Recapture Before You Buy

The recapture liability should be modeled at acquisition. Not at exit, not mid-hold, at the moment you decide to claim 100% bonus depreciation on a new asset.

An operator who enters a medical equipment acquisition knowing the asset's cost basis, the expected FMV range at a projected year-three or year-four exit, their anticipated ordinary income rate in that future year, and whether a replacement purchase will be available can calculate the net tax position across the full investment cycle before committing a dollar of capital. That's a solvable equation at purchase. At sale, it's a bill.

The practical workflow is straightforward: project exit year, estimate FMV at exit, subtract adjusted basis (likely zero if fully bonus-depreciated), apply the applicable ordinary income rate to the recapture amount, subtract the offsetting §168(k) deduction from any planned replacement, and net the result against cumulative tax savings from years of sheltered operating income.

This is how [active deductions for high-income business owners](mddeductions.com/blog/business-owner-tax-reduction-active-deductions) compound correctly, as a system modeled across the full hold, not as a one-year deduction with a deferred reckoning.
