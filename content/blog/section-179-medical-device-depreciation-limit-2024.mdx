Most articles about the **section 179 medical device depreciation limit 2024** stop at the annual ceiling and move on. That leaves medical equipment owners without the comparison they actually need: whether §179 or §168(k) bonus depreciation delivers a larger first-year deduction for their specific equipment cost and income profile. The answer depends on mechanics most practitioners understate, and getting it wrong means leaving a six-figure deduction on the table.

## The Section 179 Medical Device Depreciation Limit in 2024

For the 2024 tax year, the §179 deduction ceiling was $1,220,000. That figure applies to the total cost of qualifying property placed in service during the year, not per asset, per taxpayer.

### How the Annual Cap and Phase-Out Work

The cap is only half the constraint. Once total equipment placed in service exceeded $3,050,000 for the 2024 tax year, the §179 deduction phased out dollar-for-dollar. A practice that placed $3,250,000 of equipment into service in 2024 saw its maximum §179 deduction reduced to $1,020,000, not $1,220,000. Exceed the threshold by the full cap amount and §179 disappears entirely. For buyers of high-cost capital equipment, this phase-out is the decisive limitation, not the headline number.

### What Counts as Qualified Medical Equipment

Most diagnostic and therapeutic devices, imaging systems, infusion equipment, hyperbaric chambers, EBOO therapy units, plasmapheresis systems, qualify as 5-year MACRS property under IRS Rev. Proc. 87-56 asset class tables. That classification makes them eligible for both §179 and §168(k) elections in the year placed in service. The MACRS 5-year recovery period catches many first-time equipment buyers off guard; they assume medical hardware carries a longer depreciation life and miss the election window entirely. It doesn't. Eligibility is broad. The constraint is the cap, not the equipment category.

## Section 168(k) Bonus Depreciation: The Full-Cost Alternative

Section 168(k) operates on a completely different structure. There is no per-taxpayer dollar ceiling. The deduction is percentage-based on the asset's full acquisition cost, applied in year one.

### The Step-Down Schedule and Where It Stands Now

For property placed in service during the 2024 tax year, the §168(k) bonus rate was 60%. A $2,000,000 plasmapheresis system placed in service in 2024 generated a $1,200,000 first-year deduction under §168(k), already exceeding the §179 ceiling before the phase-out math even runs. See the [§168(k) bonus depreciation for medical equipment](mddeductions.com/blog/section-168k-bonus-depreciation-medical-equipment) breakdown for the full mechanical detail.

The step-down schedule has now largely run its course. The rate was 80% in 2023, 60% in 2024, 40% in 2025, and under current law it is 20% in 2026 before reaching zero for property placed in service after 2026. If you are evaluating equipment acquisitions now, the 20% rate is what applies. The urgency framing in most §168(k) marketing material referenced 2024 and 2025; that window is behind you. The 2026 rate still produces a meaningful first-year deduction on high-cost assets, but the arithmetic has changed.

## Section 179 vs. §168(k): A Direct Comparison for Medical Equipment Owners

This is the analysis most search results skip. Both methods accelerate depreciation. They do not behave the same way.

### Income Limitation vs. No Income Limitation

Section 179 cannot create a net operating loss. Under IRC §179(b)(3), the deduction is capped at the taxpayer's active business income for the year. Any §179 deduction that exceeds taxable business income is carried forward, it doesn't disappear, but it doesn't offset income immediately either.

Section 168(k) has no such restriction. Bonus depreciation can produce a net operating loss, which can then be carried back or forward under standard NOL rules. For a high-income physician or business owner with substantial W-2 or active pass-through income, §168(k) can shelter income well beyond the equipment's cost, the only ceiling is the asset's acquisition price times the applicable bonus rate.

This single distinction drives most of the strategic decision-making between the two methods.

### Active vs. Passive, Why the Business Structure Matters

Neither §179 nor §168(k) deductions are usable in year one if the income they offset is classified as passive under IRC §469. Passive losses can only offset passive income; they cannot shelter wages or active business income. Medical equipment owners who hold devices in a passive investment structure, no operational involvement, no employees, no active participation, face the same limitation under both methods.

The solution is genuine operating substance. MH Services LLC operates across six live medical equipment locations, each employing nurses and technicians and generating active revenue. That structure is what gives clients the real operational footprint the IRS requires for these elections to withstand scrutiny. A bare equipment-holding entity with no staff, no billing activity, and no active participation fails the test regardless of which IRC section is elected. ## Choosing the Right Election Strategy for Your Equipment Type

The decision logic is sequential, not either/or.

### Stacking Both Methods, When It Makes Sense

The optimal approach for high-cost equipment: apply §168(k) first against the full acquisition cost, then apply §179 to any remaining depreciable basis if the numbers justify it. In practice, for assets well above the §179 phase-out threshold, §168(k) does most of the work and §179 adds little. The stacking play matters most when equipment cost sits below the phase-out threshold and the buyer has a specific income target they want to hit exactly in year one.

Both elections are made on [Form 4562](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-4562) and are binding for that tax year. You cannot amend the election method after filing without IRS consent.

For high-cost devices specifically, plasmapheresis systems, EBOO therapy units, hyperbaric chambers, the acquisition cost routinely exceeds the §179 phase-out threshold, which makes §168(k) structurally superior by default. The [plasmapheresis equipment deduction guide](mddeductions.com/blog/plasmapheresis-business-tax-deduction-guide) and [EBOO therapy equipment tax deduction](mddeductions.com/blog/eboo-therapy-equipment-business-deduction) pages walk through device-specific election examples. The [hyperbaric chamber depreciation strategy](mddeductions.com/blog/hyperbaric-chamber-business-tax-deduction) covers the same decision logic for that equipment type.

§179 still wins in edge cases: equipment cost just under the phase-out threshold, combined with a buyer whose active income is modest enough that generating an NOL carries no tax benefit. If you can't use an NOL, the carry-forward mechanics of §179 may be no worse than a stranded bonus depreciation loss.

## Tax Year Timing and Documentation Requirements

The placed-in-service date controls everything. Equipment must be operational before December 31 of the tax year, purchased and delivered is not sufficient. The IRS standard is that the asset is in a condition or state of readiness and availability for its intended use. A device sitting in a crate in a storage room on December 31 does not qualify.

Documentation must support three facts: acquisition cost, placed-in-service date, and active business use. Maintain the purchase invoice, delivery confirmation, installation records, and any initial operational log or patient service record showing the device was used in active business operations before year-end. For medical equipment in a structured operating location, active participation records, staff schedules, billing records, service agreements, reinforce that income generated is active, not passive.

Thin documentation is the most common reason these deductions face IRS challenge. Keep the file complete before you file, not after.

## Which Approach Maximizes Your First-Year Medical Device Deduction?

The decision tree is direct. Large active income plus high equipment cost: §168(k) first, applied to the full asset cost at the applicable bonus rate, with §179 as a secondary tool only if basis remains and the income limitation doesn't bind. Smaller active income or equipment cost well within the §179 cap and phase-out range: §179 may produce an equivalent or simpler result, particularly if generating an NOL carries no near-term tax value.

In 2026, with the bonus rate at 20%, the gap between §168(k) and §179 narrows for lower-cost assets. For a $500,000 device, §168(k) at 20% yields $100,000, a number §179 can match if income is sufficient and cost stays below the phase-out. For a $3,000,000 device, §168(k) at 20% still yields $600,000 against a §179 ceiling that phases out entirely at that acquisition cost. The structural advantage of §168(k) persists at high asset values regardless of where the bonus rate sits.
