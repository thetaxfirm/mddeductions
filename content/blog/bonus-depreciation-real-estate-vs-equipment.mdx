The question of **bonus depreciation real estate vs equipment** comes down to one number: the MACRS recovery period. If an asset's recovery period exceeds 20 years, §168(k) is off the table. That single threshold explains why a sophisticated tax strategy built around medical equipment outperforms a real estate play on the depreciation side, structurally, not situationally.

## How the IRS Classifies Assets, and Why It Determines Everything

### Real Property vs. Personal Property: The Core Distinction

The IRC does not care what an asset looks like. It cares how the asset is classified. That classification, not the physical nature of the purchase, is the gating question for every downstream depreciation decision.

Real property covers buildings, structural components, and land improvements tied permanently to a structure. Tangible personal property covers machinery, equipment, and movable assets used in a trade or business. The two tracks have different recovery periods, different depreciation methods, and critically, different §168(k) eligibility rules.

Get the classification wrong and the deduction timeline changes by decades, not years.

## §168(k) Eligibility: Equipment Qualifies, Most Real Estate Doesn't

### What Makes an Asset §168(k)-Eligible

Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act framework, [§168(k) bonus depreciation for medical equipment](https://mddeductions.com/blog/section-168k-bonus-depreciation-medical-equipment) applies when an asset meets three tests: it must be depreciable tangible property, it must be used in a qualified trade or business, and, the hard filter, it must have a [MACRS](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) recovery period of 20 years or less.

Medical equipment, including therapeutic devices like plasmapheresis and EBOO systems, carries a 5-year MACRS recovery period under Rev. Proc. 87-56. It clears the 20-year threshold by a wide margin.

### Where Real Estate Falls Short

Residential rental property sits at 27.5 years. Nonresidential commercial real property sits at 39 years. Both fail the §168(k) eligibility test, not because of any special rule, but because IRC §168(k)(2)(A)(i) requires a recovery period of 20 years or less, and neither asset class comes close.

The gap is not marginal. A 39-year asset versus a 5-year asset is not a timing difference. It's a structural exclusion.

## Depreciation Recovery Period Comparison: Equipment vs. Real Estate

The numbers tell the story directly:

| Asset Type | MACRS Recovery Period | §168(k) Eligible? |
|---|---|---|
| Medical equipment (therapeutic devices) | 5 years | Yes |
| General machinery and equipment | 5–7 years | Yes |
| Qualified Improvement Property (QIP) | 15 years | Yes |
| Residential rental property | 27.5 years | No |
| Nonresidential real property | 39 years | No |

Even without bonus depreciation, the equipment depreciation rate comparison favors personal property. A 5-year asset deducts 20% of its basis per year on a straight-line schedule. A 39-year asset deducts roughly 2.6% per year. Layer §168(k) on top and the equipment owner takes the full deduction in year one. The real estate investor is still waiting.

This is why the depreciation recovery period for equipment vs. real estate matters beyond the bonus depreciation question. Shorter recovery periods accelerate the deduction schedule regardless of any bonus election.

## Real Estate Bonus Depreciation Limits in Practice

### Qualified Improvement Property: The One Real Estate Exception

Real estate investors have a workaround: cost segregation studies. A cost segregation study reclassifies portions of a commercial building's cost from 39-year real property into 5- or 7-year personal property (fixtures, finishes, land improvements). Those reclassified components then qualify for §168(k).

It works, but the math limits it. A well-executed cost segregation study on a commercial building typically reclassifies 20–30% of the total project cost as short-lived personal property eligible for bonus depreciation. The remaining 70–80% stays on the 39-year schedule, outside §168(k) entirely. You need a formal study, which adds cost and time, and you still only unlock a fraction of the asset's value.

Qualified Improvement Property (QIP), interior improvements to nonresidential buildings, sits at a 15-year recovery period after the TCJA technical correction. QIP qualifies for §168(k), making it the other real estate carve-out. But QIP applies to a specific and narrow category of improvements; it does not apply to the building itself or to new construction.

Neither cost segregation nor QIP delivers the clean, full-value §168(k) deduction that purpose-built equipment ownership delivers. They are partial plays on top of a fundamentally longer-recovery asset. For context on how §179 interacts with these strategies, see [§179 expensing limits for medical devices](https://mddeductions.com/blog/section-179-medical-device-depreciation-limit-2024).

## Personal Property Tax Advantages for Operating Businesses

### Active Income Offset: The Mechanic That Matters for High-Earners

The personal property tax advantages of equipment go beyond recovery periods. The more important mechanic for high-income owners is the active vs. passive income distinction under IRC §469.

Passive real estate losses, including those generated by cost segregation, are generally suspended for taxpayers who don't qualify as real estate professionals. They carry forward until you generate passive income or sell the asset. For a high-income W-2 earner or business owner, the deduction doesn't land when you need it most.

Equipment deployed in an active operating business works differently. [Active income offset through medical equipment ownership](https://mddeductions.com/blog/active-income-medical-equipment-business-168k-bonus-depreciation) requires that the business have real substance: staff, patient revenue, billing, and ongoing operations. When those elements are present, the §168(k) deduction offsets active income, W-2, 1099, or pass-through business income, in the year the asset is placed in service. No carry-forward. No passive loss basket.

MH Services LLC, which operates across six live locations deploying therapeutic protocols including Plasmapheresis, EBOO, and TBI treatments, staffed with nurses and technicians generating active patient revenue, is the model for what that operating substance looks like. That structure is what converts a §168(k) deduction from a paper position into a defensible one. See [IRS operating business requirements for medical devices](https://mddeductions.com/blog/medical-device-operating-business-requirements-irs) for what the IRS expects in practice.

For high-income owners, this is the structural advantage: the deduction hits in year one, against the income that's already the problem.

## Strategic Asset Acquisition: Choosing Equipment Over (or Alongside) Real Estate

Real estate and equipment strategies are not mutually exclusive. Many owners hold both. But their tax timing and income-offset mechanics differ materially, and conflating them leads to misaligned expectations.

For a high-income owner whose primary goal is reducing year-one tax liability, the decision framework is straightforward:

- **Equipment in an active operating business:** Full §168(k) deduction in year one, offsets active income, no study required, no passive loss limitation.
- **Real estate with cost segregation:** Partial §168(k) on reclassified components, passive loss risk for most high-earners, requires a formal study, majority of asset value outside bonus depreciation.
- **Real estate without cost segregation:** No §168(k) at all. Straight-line depreciation over 27.5 or 39 years.

If the goal is year-one tax impact, the equipment path is more direct. Real estate builds equity and can generate its own returns, but as a depreciation strategy, it requires more scaffolding to produce what equipment ownership delivers by default.

Equipment ownership carries its own exit considerations. When the asset is eventually sold, prior depreciation is subject to recapture.
