A bonus depreciation deduction on medical equipment is only as good as the income category it offsets. Buy the equipment, place it in service, take the deduction, and if you fail the material participation requirements for your medical equipment business, that deduction doesn't disappear. It converts to a suspended passive loss, sitting on your return until you generate passive income to absorb it or dispose of the activity. For a high-income owner counting on that deduction to reduce W-2 income this year, that's the difference between a real tax outcome and a paper loss with no current use.

This is the mechanical core of IRC §469: a loss from a trade or business is only currently deductible against active income (wages, business income, capital gains) if you materially participate in that business. If you don't, the loss is passive, and passive losses can only offset passive income. Most owners in this position have none.

## Why Material Participation Determines Whether Your Deduction Is Real

The material participation test under IRC §469(h) exists to separate active operators from passive investors. Congress built the passive activity loss rules to stop taxpayers from buying into ventures for the tax loss alone, with no operational involvement. If you're structuring a medical equipment purchase to offset active income, the IRS wants proof you're running a business, not parking money in a tax shelter.

### Active Income vs. Passive Income in an Equipment Business

Active income includes wages, self-employment income, and gains from a business you materially participate in. Passive income comes from rental activities and businesses where you don't materially participate, no matter how much cash flow they generate. The category isn't about how the money is earned. It's about your level of involvement in earning it.

A medical equipment business bought purely as a depreciation vehicle, with no owner involvement past signing the purchase agreement, produces a passive loss by default. That loss sits suspended under the [passive activity loss rules under IRC §469](https://mddeductions.com/blog/passive-activity-loss-medical-equipment-irc-469) until you either generate passive income elsewhere or dispose of the activity in a taxable transaction. Neither is a realistic plan for an owner trying to reduce this year's tax bill.

## The Seven IRC §469(h) Tests, Ranked by Realism for an Absentee Owner

Treasury Regulation §1.469-5T lists seven ways to establish material participation. Most were written with a farmer, a retail owner, or a full-time operator in mind, someone whose day job is the business. The seven tests, briefly:

1. More than 500 hours of participation during the year.
2. Your participation constitutes substantially all the participation in the activity by anyone, including employees.
3. More than 100 hours of participation, and no one else, including employees, participates more than you.
4. The activity is a "significant participation activity," and your combined hours across all such activities exceed 500 for the year.
5. Material participation for any five of the preceding ten tax years.
6. The activity is a personal service activity, and you materially participated in any three prior years.
7. Based on facts and circumstances, you participate on a regular, continuous, and substantial basis. This test only applies if you clear at least 100 hours.

Tests 1, 2, 5, and 6 assume you're either running daily operations yourself or have years of prior history in the activity. That doesn't describe a high-income owner, a physician, an executive, a specialist with a full-time career, who is buying into a staffed medical equipment operation as a business owner, not a clinician on the floor. IRC §469(h) lists seven distinct tests for material participation, but for an absentee owner running a staffed clinic, only two are realistic candidates: the 100-hour/no-one-else-more test and the facts-and-circumstances test.

### The 100-Hour Test Explained

Test 3 requires two things: you log more than 100 hours of participation in the activity during the tax year, and no other individual, including any employee, participates more hours than you. Note what it doesn't require. It doesn't require you to work more hours than the total combined staff, only more than any single person. If your clinic manager works 900 hours and no other individual employee exceeds your 105 hours, you clear the test on that prong.

This is the test built for an owner who oversees rather than operates. It rewards concentrated, decision-level time rather than volume of labor.

### The Facts-and-Circumstances Test Explained

Test 7 is the backstop. If you clear at least 100 hours but can't confirm the "no one else participates more" condition with certainty, the facts-and-circumstances test asks whether your participation is regular, continuous, and substantial, evaluated on the whole picture rather than a hard threshold. It's less mechanical than the 100-hour test, which makes it harder to rely on alone but useful as a secondary argument if your hours land in a gray zone.

Neither test asks you to be at a clinic every day. Both ask you to be substantively involved in running the business.

### What Counts as Participation Hours

Management-level activity counts, provided it's genuine decision-making rather than passive receipt of information:

- Hiring and firing decisions for clinic staff and management.
- Vendor selection and contract negotiation for equipment, supplies, or services.
- Monthly or quarterly financial statement review with documented follow-up questions or directives.
- Compliance sign-off: reviewing and approving policies, licensure renewals, or regulatory filings.
- Site visits where you inspect operations, meet staff, and issue action items.
- Strategic decisions on expansion, equipment purchases, or service line changes.

An owner who spends 105 hours a year on vendor negotiation, compliance sign-off, and financial review for a plasmapheresis clinic, while a hired nurse manager handles daily operations, can satisfy the 100-hour test if no single employee logs more hours on management-level activity. That's the model built for someone with a demanding primary career.

### What Doesn't Count

Regulation §1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii) excludes work not customarily done by owners in that type of activity, and it treats certain investor-type activity as non-qualifying unless you're also involved in day-to-day management. That means:

- Reading financial statements without acting on them.
- Attending an annual meeting with no operational follow-up.
- Reviewing reports prepared by others without documented decisions resulting from that review.
- Time spent purely monitoring the investment, as opposed to directing it.

The line is action versus observation. Passive review of numbers is investor behavior. Review that produces a decision, a vendor change, a staffing adjustment, a corrective action, is participation.

## Documentation That Proves Material Participation on Audit

The IRS doesn't take your word for hours worked. On audit, the burden is on you to substantiate participation with records, and the quality of those records determines whether the deduction survives.

### Building a Contemporaneous Log

A contemporaneous log, dated, itemized, and tied to specific management decisions, is the single most-cited factor in Tax Court cases where taxpayers won or lost material participation disputes. "Contemporaneous" means logged near the time the activity occurred, not reconstructed from memory after an audit notice arrives. Each entry should include the date, duration, and a description specific enough to tie to a real decision. "Reviewed Q2 P&L, directed 8% reduction in supply vendor spend" carries far more weight than "management review."

### Records the IRS Will Actually Request

Beyond the log itself, examiners typically ask for supporting documents that corroborate the hours claimed:

- Calendars showing site visits, board calls, and vendor meetings.
- Board or management meeting minutes with your attendance and input recorded.
- Emails with timestamps showing decisions you made or directed.
- Signed decision memos: hiring approvals, vendor contracts, compliance sign-offs.
- Travel records tied to site visits, if applicable.

Tax professionals who handle passive-loss exams routinely note that the IRS scrutinizes material participation claims most heavily when a return shows a large first-year loss offsetting W-2 or other active income. That's exactly the profile of a bonus depreciation medical equipment purchase, and it makes documentation, not intent, the deciding factor. ## Common Mistakes That Turn an Active Business Into a Passive Loss Trap

The most frequent failure is treating the business as fully outsourced: hiring a management company or clinical staff to run everything and never engaging in governance decisions yourself. If you can't point to hours where you directed the business, the 100-hour test fails regardless of how the paperwork is titled.

A second common mistake is waiting until an audit notice arrives to build the log. Reconstructed records, assembled from memory months or years later, carry far less weight than contemporaneous entries and invite skepticism from examiners who've seen the pattern before.

A third mistake is misclassifying hours as investor-type activity under Reg. §1.469-5T(f)(2)(ii): logging time spent reading reports or attending calls without documenting the decisions that resulted. Hours without action attached don't count, and an examiner will draw that distinction quickly.

Finally, some owners assume any involvement counts, without tracking whether an employee's hours exceed theirs. If your clinic manager or another single employee logs more management-level hours than you do, the 100-hour test fails on that prong alone, pushing you toward the facts-and-circumstances test, which carries far more uncertainty.
