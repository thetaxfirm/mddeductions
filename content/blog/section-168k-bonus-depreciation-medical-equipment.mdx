Section 168(k) bonus depreciation medical equipment is one of the few tax provisions that can move a high-cost capital acquisition entirely off your taxable income in the year you place it in service. The mechanics are real, but so are the conditions, and most guides stop at the deduction percentage without explaining what actually makes it stick. This guide covers the statutory requirements, the passive activity rules that determine whether the deduction is usable, the operating model substance the IRS expects to see, and the recapture risk that serious buyers need to model before they close.

## What §168(k) Bonus Depreciation Actually Does

### The First-Year Deduction Mechanic

Under [IRC §168(k)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/168), qualifying property placed in service during the tax year can be fully expensed in that year rather than depreciated over its MACRS recovery period. For medical equipment, that means an asset with a 5- or 7-year MACRS class life doesn't get spread across five or seven tax returns, the deduction hits year one.

This matters because deductions have time value. A dollar of deduction in year one is worth more than the same dollar spread over seven years, especially for taxpayers in the 37% federal bracket. Place qualifying property in service, meet the statutory tests, and the entire depreciable basis is deductible against your income in that tax year.

### How the 2026 Depreciation Rate Is Determined

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 established 100% bonus depreciation and then built in a phase-down schedule under §168(k)(6). The rate stepped down to 80% for property placed in service in 2023, then 60% in 2024, and 40% in 2025. For qualifying property placed in service in 2026, the applicable rate is 20% under current law.

That 20% still represents meaningful acceleration for high-cost equipment. A $500,000 imaging or therapy system generates a $100,000 first-year bonus deduction on top of whatever regular MACRS depreciation applies to the remaining basis. Model the actual number before drawing conclusions about deal economics. The remaining 80% of basis continues on the normal MACRS schedule.

Congress has periodically revisited bonus depreciation rates, so verify the applicable rate under current law before finalizing projections. The statutory schedule under §168(k)(6) is the controlling authority.

## §168(k) Equipment Deduction Requirements: What Medical Devices Must Satisfy

### Original Use and New Property Rules

Four statutory requirements determine whether property qualifies for bonus depreciation.

**First**, the property must be "qualified property", generally, MACRS property with a recovery period of 20 years or less. Medical equipment (imaging units, infusion systems, plasmapheresis machines, EBOO systems) typically falls in the 5-year MACRS asset class under Rev. Proc. 87-56, clearing this hurdle cleanly.

**Second**, original-use rules apply. For new property, the original use must begin with the taxpayer. You can't claim bonus depreciation on used equipment unless it qualifies under the used-property acquisition rules added by TCJA. Used property qualifies if: it wasn't previously used by the taxpayer or a predecessor; it wasn't acquired from a related party under §267 or §707(b); and it wasn't acquired in a carry-over basis transaction. A newly purchased plasmapheresis unit from an unrelated manufacturer satisfies the original-use test without issue.

**Third**, the property must be placed in service before December 31 of the tax year for 2026 deductions.

**Fourth**, written binding contract rules govern acquisition timing. If a written binding contract to acquire the property existed before September 28, 2017, it falls under prior law. For all modern equipment purchases, confirm there is no pre-existing binding contract that would push the property out of the TCJA regime.

### Placed-in-Service Timing and the Acquisition Date Test

"Placed in service" means the equipment is in a condition or state of readiness and availability for its intended use, not just purchased, not just delivered, but operationally ready. For a clinical device, this means it's installed, calibrated, and available to treat patients or clients within your tax year.

Acquisition date matters independently. Under §168(k), property subject to a written binding contract is treated as acquired on the contract date. If you sign a purchase agreement in November 2026 but the unit isn't delivered until March 2027, the property is placed in service in 2027, and you take the 2027-applicable rate, not 2026's. Coordinate delivery and installation timelines deliberately if year-end placement is the goal.

## Active Business Income: Why Passive Owners Get Shut Out

### Material Participation Tests Under §469

A bonus depreciation deduction creates a tax loss in year one if it exceeds the business's current revenue. That loss is only deductible against your other income if the activity is non-passive. Under §469, losses from passive activities can only offset passive income, they can't reduce W-2 wages, self-employment income, or portfolio income.

The IRS has consistently held that bonus depreciation deductions hinge not just on property qualification but on the taxpayer's ability to absorb the loss against non-passive income. A passive investor who funds a medical equipment operation but doesn't materially participate ends up with a suspended loss, one that sits on the books until either passive income appears or the activity is disposed of in a qualifying transaction.

Material participation is determined by seven tests under Treas. Reg. §1.469-5T. You satisfy material participation if any one of these applies:

1. You participate more than 500 hours in the activity during the year.
2. Your participation constitutes substantially all the participation in the activity.
3. You participate more than 100 hours and no other individual participates more.
4. The activity is a significant participation activity and your combined significant participation activities exceed 500 hours.
5. You materially participated in the activity for any 5 of the prior 10 tax years.
6. The activity is a personal service activity in which you materially participated for any 3 prior tax years.
7. Based on all facts and circumstances, you participate on a regular, continuous, and substantial basis.

High-income owners most commonly qualify under Test 1 (500+ hours) or Test 7 (facts and circumstances). Test 5 works for established operators who've previously run the business. Tests 2 and 3 are available for smaller operations where the owner is the primary active participant.

### Structuring the Operating Entity to Qualify

A paper-only structure fails here. Forming an LLC, acquiring equipment, and then delegating all operations to third parties doesn't establish material participation. The owner needs documented, verifiable time spent on real business activities, patient intake oversight, billing management, marketing, vendor relationships, clinical staff supervision, logged contemporaneously.

The operating entity structure matters too. A single-member LLC or S-corp where the owner-operator runs the business directly is straightforward. A limited partnership structure, by contrast, presumptively treats limited partners as passive, a structural choice that requires careful review before acquisition.

## Medical Device Depreciation Tax Strategy: Building a Compliant Operating Model

### Staff, Revenue, and Substance Requirements

The IRS applies substance-over-form scrutiny to large first-year deductions. An entity that owns medical equipment but has no clinical staff, no active patient or client billing, and no physical location doesn't look like an operating business, it looks like a tax shelter. That distinction matters at audit.

Real operations mean: employed or contracted clinical staff delivering services, a licensed physical location where services are provided, active billing and revenue collection from clients or patients, and a P&L that reflects genuine business activity. Operating across multiple clinic locations, each with employed clinical staff and active client billing, establishes the operational footprint that satisfies both the material participation tests under §469 and the substance-over-form scrutiny that accompanies large first-year deductions.

### Documentation the IRS Expects

If examined, the IRS will ask for:

- **Equipment purchase agreements** showing acquisition date, price, and seller identity
- **Placed-in-service records**, installation completion dates, calibration logs, any certificates of readiness
- **Time logs** documenting owner hours and activities supporting material participation, maintained contemporaneously, not reconstructed
- **P&L statements** showing active revenue tied to the equipment's use
- **Payroll records** confirming employed clinical staff
- **Lease or property records** for the physical operating location
- **Depreciation schedules** with the correct MACRS class life and bonus depreciation calculation

Gaps in this documentation stack are where audits become expensive. Build the file as you build the operation, not after the fact.

## Depreciation Recapture on Medical Equipment: The Risk Most Guides Skip

§1245 recapture is the exit risk that most bonus depreciation write-ups bury or omit. When you sell or dispose of personal property on which you claimed bonus depreciation, the IRS recaptures the previously deducted amount as ordinary income, not capital gain, to the extent of the gain.

Here's the mechanic: you acquire a $500,000 therapy system and deduct $100,000 in year-one bonus depreciation (at the 2026 20% rate). Your adjusted basis drops to $400,000 (before further MACRS depreciation). If you sell the equipment two years later for $350,000, the gain attributable to those depreciation deductions is recaptured at ordinary income rates under §1245, which can reach 37% federally.

For high-cost medical devices, the recapture exposure is material. A full-basis, 100%-depreciation year (as existed in prior years) followed by a sale before the end of the MACRS recovery period could erase a significant portion of the original tax benefit, depending on sale price and holding period.

This interacts directly with exit planning. If you intend to sell the operating entity or the equipment within the MACRS recovery period, model the recapture. Like-kind exchanges under §1031 don't apply to personal property, so §1245 recapture on medical equipment is a cash tax event at disposition, not something you can roll over.

## Applying the Strategy: Plasmapheresis, EBOO, and Cash-Pay Medical Equipment in 2026

Plasmapheresis and EBOO (extracorporeal blood oxygenation and ozonation) therapy systems are capital-intensive acquisitions, unit costs for clinical-grade systems are substantial, and they sit within defined MACRS class lives, making them textbook candidates for §168(k) treatment when placed in service within the tax year, provided the operating entity clears the material participation threshold.

The cash-pay model that characterizes these modalities has practical advantages for the depreciation strategy. Revenue comes directly from self-pay clients rather than insurance reimbursement, so there's no accounts receivable delay from payer adjudication and revenue recognition is cleaner. That active revenue, flowing from day one of operations, supports both the P&L substance the IRS expects and the material participation hour log the owner needs to maintain.

Demand for these therapy modalities has expanded through wellness and longevity medicine markets in 2026, which means the business case doesn't rest entirely on the tax position. Operations can stand on their own revenue model while the §168(k) deduction accelerates the after-tax return in year one.

For high-income W-2 earners, self-employed physicians, or business owners with active income to absorb losses, the combination of a defined MACRS class life, an identifiable placed-in-service date, high acquisition cost, and cash-pay revenue makes this equipment category particularly responsive to a well-structured bonus depreciation strategy.
