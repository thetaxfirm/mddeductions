The medical device operating business requirements IRS examiners apply aren't published in a single checklist, they're assembled from IRC §469, Treasury regulations, audit guidelines, and Tax Court precedent. If you're structuring a medical equipment business to generate active deductions, you need to know exactly what the IRS looks for before an exam starts, not after.

## What the IRS Means by a 'Real' Operating Business

The IRS does not treat asset ownership as a business. Buying equipment, placing it in an LLC, and claiming depreciation is not enough. The agency's operational substance doctrine holds that a legitimate business must have real economic activity: people rendering services, customers paying for them, and records that prove both.

### Active vs. Shelter: How the IRS Draws the Line

The line between an active business and a tax shelter runs through IRC §469. Passive activities, those in which the taxpayer does not materially participate, cannot offset active income. Losses pile up as suspended passive losses until the activity is disposed of.

IRS exam teams flag paper-only structures using a predictable pattern: no payroll filings, no third-party revenue, no owner activity logs. When those three elements are missing, the structure looks like a shelter regardless of the entity documents. The [active income medical equipment business under §168(k)](mddeductions.com/blog/active-income-medical-equipment-business-168k-bonus-depreciation) distinction is the threshold question in any exam of this type.

### Why Medical Equipment Businesses Get Extra Scrutiny

High-cost medical devices generate large first-year deductions under §168(k) and §179, which means they attract examiner attention. The IRS knows that equipment businesses are a common vehicle for passive loss sheltering. A business running Plasmapheresis, EBOO, or TBI protocols with licensed staff and active billing looks fundamentally different from one with only a purchase agreement and a depreciation schedule. Examiners know the difference, and so should you.

## Staffing Requirements: Employees, Roles, and Payroll Substance

A medical device operating business needs humans performing services. That means licensed clinicians administering treatments and at minimum billing or administrative staff managing patient flow.

### Who Counts as Qualified Staff

Licensed nurses, nurse practitioners, or medical technicians who administer IV-based or device-driven therapies are the clinical core. Their licensure matters, it establishes that actual regulated medical services are being rendered, not simulated activity. Front-desk and billing staff complete the operational picture. A single contractor who occasionally shows up does not establish employment infrastructure.

### Employment Records the IRS Expects

Payroll tax filings are your primary credibility signal. Form 941 (quarterly payroll) and Form 940 (annual FUTA) tell an examiner that real wages are being paid to real people. Missing or late 940/941 filings signal that payroll is reconstructed, not contemporaneous. Beyond filings, the IRS expects:

- W-2s and 1099s issued to workers
- State unemployment tax filings
- Workers' compensation coverage documentation
- Offer letters or employment agreements for key clinical roles

## Material Participation Requirements: IRS Definition and the 500-Hour Test

IRC §469(h) defines material participation as involvement that is "regular, continuous, and substantial." The IRS has consistently interpreted this to require contemporaneous documentation, not reconstructed estimates prepared at tax time. Understanding the material participation requirements IRS definition is non-negotiable if you're claiming active treatment of business losses.

### The Seven Tests, and Which One Applies to You

Under Treasury Regulation §1.469-5T, a taxpayer qualifies as a material participant by meeting any one of seven tests. Two are most relevant to equipment business owners:

- **Primary test (500 hours):** You participated in the activity for at least 500 hours during the tax year.
- **100-hour/no-one-else test:** You participated for at least 100 hours, and no other individual logged more hours than you.

A third test, participation in all significant participation activities totaling more than 500 hours, can apply across multiple businesses. For a single-location medical equipment business, the 500-hour primary test is the cleanest path. It leaves no ambiguity and survives scrutiny without secondary arguments.

### Contemporaneous Time Logs as Audit Evidence

"Contemporaneous" means logged as the hours occur, not assembled in April from memory. Tax Court has repeatedly rejected reconstructed participation logs. Your documentation should include:

- Daily or weekly time entries with date, duration, and specific activity description
- Calendar records, emails, or scheduling software entries corroborating the log
- No round-number entries (e.g. "8 hours every Monday" with no variation raises flags)

Digital tools, Google Calendar exports, practice management software activity logs, email timestamps, are all usable corroboration. A handwritten log with no supporting records is the weakest possible evidence.

## Billing and Revenue Documentation: Proving Economic Reality

The IRS does not accept projected revenue as evidence of economic activity. Cash must have moved. For a [cash-pay wellness equipment business model](mddeductions.com/blog/cash-pay-wellness-medical-equipment-business-model), that means actual payment receipts from actual patients, with bank deposits that match reported gross revenue.

An examiner reviewing a medical equipment business will pull:

- **Invoices and receipts** matching each reported revenue line
- **Bank statements** showing deposits that reconcile to the general ledger
- **Service agreements** or patient intake forms establishing that services were contracted and rendered
- **De-identified treatment records** or scheduling logs confirming patient visits

Electronic health record (EHR) systems and scheduling platforms create a natural audit trail, appointment records, treatment notes tied to dates, and billing entries linked to services rendered. If your business runs on paper or a spreadsheet, you're working harder than necessary to defend the revenue figure.

The gap between projected and actual revenue is one of the most common triggers for passive reclassification. If a business reports significant depreciation deductions but minimal actual revenue, examiners treat the revenue as fictional and the activity as passive.

## Operational Substance: Hours, Location, and Business Records

Physical presence is not optional. The IRS expects a medical equipment business to occupy real space, during stated hours, with evidence that the space is used for its stated purpose. See the [EBOO therapy equipment deduction guide](mddeductions.com/blog/eboo-therapy-equipment-business-deduction) for what this looks like in practice for a specific treatment modality.

### IRS Operational Substance Medical Business Checklist

When evaluating IRS operational substance medical business requirements, an examiner typically looks for:

- **Executed lease agreement** for the treatment location, in the entity's name
- **Utility bills** in the entity's name, showing active service at the address
- **Posted business hours** and evidence the location was open during those hours
- **Equipment service and maintenance records** showing the device was operational
- **Treatment logs** tied to specific dates, patient identifiers, and supervising clinician
- **Entity-level bank account** with activity consistent with reported revenue and expenses
- **Business license and any applicable state health permits**
- **Signage or directory listing** at the physical location

These records are what separates a real business from a paper structure. A filing cabinet (or a well-organized cloud drive) with these documents is the difference between a two-week audit and a three-year dispute. Understanding active deductions for business owners starts with building this infrastructure first.

## Audit-Hardening Strategies: Surviving an IRS Exam

The audit is survivable if the paper trail exists. Most passive reclassification cases collapse because the owner's documentation gaps, not the structure itself, made defense impossible. In cases where the IRS has reclassified medical or equipment businesses as passive shelters, the common thread is absent owner participation logs, no third-party payroll filings, and revenue that never materialized beyond projected figures.

Practical steps to harden the position:

**Entity-level bookkeeping.** Business finances run through the entity's own accounts. No personal account commingling. QuickBooks or equivalent, maintained monthly, not reconstructed annually.

**Annual third-party review.** A CPA or enrolled agent reviews the books, payroll filings, and activity documentation annually, ideally before tax filing. This creates a contemporaneous record that a professional evaluated the operation.

**Documented owner participation logs.** Maintained throughout the year, corroborated by calendar and email. Not assembled at tax time.

**Written operating agreement.** The LLC operating agreement should specify the owner's operational role, not just their ownership percentage. Vague documents invite examiner re-characterization.

**Depreciation position documentation.** The §168(k) bonus depreciation deduction is available only on property used in an active trade or business under IRC §168(k)(2)(A)(ii). A passive reclassification retroactively invalidates the deduction. Defending the §168(k) bonus depreciation for medical equipment position requires the active business to hold up, which means every element above must be in place before you file. The same logic applies to [Section 179 depreciation limits for medical devices](mddeductions.com/blog/section-179-medical-device-depreciation-limit-2024), where the active use requirement is equally strict.

The IRS cannot reclassify a business that demonstrably functions as one. Staffing records, payroll filings, treatment logs, bank deposits, and owner activity documentation, assembled contemporaneously and reviewed annually, are the infrastructure that makes the tax position defensible.
