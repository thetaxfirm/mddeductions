## What TBI Therapy Equipment Actually Costs

### Hardware Tiers: Entry-Level vs. Clinical-Grade TBI Systems

TBI (transcranial bioregulation and interference) systems span a wide price range depending on output power, channel count, software integration, and intended use setting. The tiers break down roughly as follows:

- **Wearable or personal-use devices**: $5,000–$25,000. Suitable for at-home protocols; not appropriate for a clinic billing premium cash-pay rates.
- **Tabletop clinical units**: $40,000–$90,000. The core device for a single-operator clinical setting. These are the entry point for any serious cash-pay deployment.
- **Full-suite clinical-grade systems**: $100,000–$250,000+. Multi-channel, software-controlled platforms with integrated patient tracking, remote monitoring, and protocols designed for high-volume throughput.

Clinical-grade TBI systems from established manufacturers typically carry list prices in the mid-five-figure range. Full turnkey deployments, including installation, calibration, and staff training, push total cost basis into the low-to-mid six figures. That full number is what matters for depreciation purposes.

### Supplier Landscape and Procurement Channels

The TBI device market in 2026 includes a mix of European medical-device manufacturers, U.S.-based wellness hardware companies, and direct-from-manufacturer procurement through licensed distributors. Key cost inputs beyond the hardware itself:

- **Installation and calibration**: $3,000–$10,000 depending on facility complexity
- **Initial staff training**: $2,000–$8,000 per technician cohort
- **Annual service contracts**: 8–12% of hardware cost per year, typically
- **Software licensing**: $200–$600/month on subscription-based platforms

All qualifying ancillary costs, installation, training, and required software, can be bundled into the depreciable cost basis under §168(k) when properly documented. That makes procurement structure a tax decision, not just a vendor negotiation.

## TBI System Cost Basis and §168(k) Bonus Depreciation

### How the Cost Basis Is Established for Depreciation

The depreciable cost basis equals the total amount paid to place the asset in service: device purchase price plus all costs required to make it operational. That means the invoice from the manufacturer, the installer's invoice, the trainer's invoice, and qualifying software, all of it adds to basis if it's necessary to place the system in productive use.

Under [§168(k) bonus depreciation for active medical equipment businesses](https://mddeductions.com/blog/active-income-medical-equipment-business-168k-bonus-depreciation), qualified property placed in service in 2026 is eligible for 40% first-year bonus depreciation under the current phase-down schedule (100% expired after 2022; the rate stepped down annually and sits at 40% for 2026 under current law). That is still a substantial first-year deduction against ordinary active income. On a $200,000 cost basis, the 40% bonus yields an $80,000 deduction in year one, with the remaining $120,000 depreciated over the 5-year MACRS schedule.

High-income operators considering [Section 179 limits on medical device depreciation](https://mddeductions.com/blog/section-179-medical-device-depreciation-limit-2024) as an alternative should note the §179 deduction cap ($1,220,000 in 2026) and its business income limitation, it cannot create a loss, while §168(k) can. For operators with high W-2 or active business income to offset, §168(k) is typically the stronger tool.

Tax practitioners who work with medical equipment businesses consistently flag operating substance, real staff, real patients, real billing, as the single most important factor distinguishing a defensible §168(k) claim from a challenged one. The deduction follows the business; the business has to be real.

### Depreciation Recapture Risk on Exit

If you sell TBI equipment before its MACRS useful life ends, you face §1245 recapture on all depreciation taken. That recapture is taxed as ordinary income, not capital gain. A $200,000 basis fully depreciated and then sold for $80,000 produces $80,000 of ordinary income in the year of sale, regardless of your holding period. Understanding the full [depreciation recapture liability on equipment sale](https://mddeductions.com/blog/depreciation-recapture-medical-equipment-sale-liability) before you exit is non-negotiable. Build a recapture model into any exit scenario from day one.

## Why Cash-Pay Patient Demand Supports This Investment

TBI therapy is structurally outside insurance reimbursement in most jurisdictions. CPT coding for transcranial bioregulation protocols is not standardized for third-party billing, which means the modality lives entirely in the cash-pay market, and that is an advantage, not a limitation.

The patient population driving demand is the same demographic already spending on longevity medicine: high-income adults aged 35–65 who are proactive about cognitive function, recovery, and performance optimization. This group is not waiting for a referral. They research protocols, pay out-of-pocket, and respond to clinic branding that positions treatment as an investment in function rather than a response to disease.

## Staffing, Operating Requirements, and IRS Substance

A TBI therapy business that passes IRS scrutiny looks like this: a physical treatment space, licensed clinical staff administering protocols, a patient intake and billing process, and records that document actual treatment delivery. What it does not look like is a device sitting in a storage unit generating paper losses.

Minimum infrastructure for a defensible active business:

- **Clinical director or supervising practitioner**: Required in most states for any device operating under medical or wellness scope. A licensed nurse practitioner, physician, or similarly credentialed operator meets this requirement.
- **Technician or care coordinator**: Handles patient intake, session scheduling, and device operation. This role can be part-time at low volume.
- **Billing and documentation system**: SOAP notes or equivalent session records, payment receipts, and patient agreements. These form the audit trail the IRS wants to see.
- **Physical location**: A dedicated treatment room, not a shared residential space.

The [IRS operating business requirements for medical devices](https://mddeductions.com/blog/medical-device-operating-business-requirements-irs) center on whether the taxpayer is genuinely in the trade or business of providing services. Material participation, typically 500+ hours of active involvement annually, is the benchmark under IRC §469. The infrastructure above makes that participation verifiable and defensible.

[Active deductions for high-income business owners](https://mddeductions.com/blog/business-owner-tax-reduction-active-deductions) depend entirely on the business being real. The equipment acquisition is the entry point; the operating infrastructure is what keeps the deduction defensible on examination.
