## Why Six Locations Change the Tax Math

### One Location Is a Business; Six Locations Are a System

A single-location wellness business can generate meaningful §168(k) deductions. But a single site concentrates every risk: one lease, one revenue stream, one pool of staff hours for material participation purposes, and one equipment purchase event.

Six locations restructure all of that. Each site adds a discrete equipment acquisition, a separate payroll record, a distinct lease, and additional management hours that flow toward the 500-hour material participation threshold under IRC §469. The IRS tests active trade or business status at the activity level, and a six-site operation generates operational activity across every dimension an examiner uses to evaluate substance.

The structural logic is straightforward: scale creates evidence. More sites mean more leases, more staff W-2s, more site visit logs, and more revenue traceable to specific locations. Each of those data points is a brick in the active-business defense.

## Multi-Location Medical Business Structure: Entity and Governance Choices

### Single LLC vs. Series LLC vs. Subsidiary Structure

Entity design for a multiple location medical business structure involves a direct trade-off between consolidated depreciation flow-through and liability ring-fencing.

**Single operating LLC:** All six sites operate under one entity. Depreciation flows directly to the member's Schedule E. Simple to administer, but a liability event at one site reaches the entire entity's assets.

**Series LLC (available in select states):** Each location sits in a separate series with statutory liability segregation inside one filing. Potentially cleaner ring-fencing, but series LLC treatment is still unsettled under federal tax law, the IRS has not issued final guidance on whether each series is a separate entity for tax purposes, which creates risk in multi-state operations.

**Parent-subsidiary structure:** A parent LLC owns six single-member subsidiary LLCs, one per site. The subsidiaries are disregarded entities for federal tax purposes, so depreciation consolidates at the parent level. Liability stays ring-fenced by entity boundary. This structure also supports clean W-2 payroll records at each subsidiary, a detail that matters during examination. For most multi-site wellness operators, this is the most defensible architecture.

### How to Allocate Revenue and Profit Across Sites

Revenue allocation is not just an accounting exercise, it directly affects how active income offsets W-2 income from other sources.

Each subsidiary's revenue should map to services delivered at that site. Intercompany management fees between the parent and subsidiaries must reflect fair market value and be documented in a management services agreement. Profit that flows up to the parent is active business income if material participation is met at the activity level. That income, or the losses generated by first-year §168(k) deductions, can offset high W-2 compensation in the year the equipment is placed in service, provided the activity qualifies as non-passive under IRC §469.

## Scaling Equipment Depreciation Across Six Locations

### Stacking §168(k) Deductions Site by Site

Under [§168(k) bonus depreciation for medical equipment](https://mddeductions.com/blog/section-168k-bonus-depreciation-medical-equipment), qualified property placed in service in the tax year is eligible for immediate expensing up to the applicable bonus percentage. Each location's equipment acquisition is a separate placed-in-service event, six locations means six distinct deduction opportunities in the same tax year.

A single EBOO or Plasmapheresis unit can carry an asset cost well into six figures. Across six owned locations, the aggregate §168(k)-eligible equipment base compounds that deduction at scale. If each site's primary treatment unit costs $150,000 and all six are placed in service in the same year, the aggregate eligible basis approaches $900,000 before adding ancillary equipment. Multiply that across a full build-out including TBI units, infusion chairs, monitoring equipment, and fit-out items that qualify as personal property, and the first-year deduction potential is substantial. See the [EBOO therapy equipment deduction mechanics](https://mddeductions.com/blog/eboo-therapy-equipment-business-deduction) and [Plasmapheresis business tax deduction guide](https://mddeductions.com/blog/plasmapheresis-business-tax-deduction-guide) for per-unit cost ranges and deduction mechanics on those specific asset types.

Understanding [active income mechanics under §168(k)](https://mddeductions.com/blog/active-income-medical-equipment-business-168k-bonus-depreciation) is prerequisite reading before scaling this strategy, the deduction only flows as non-passive loss if the activity clears the material participation test.

### MACRS Recovery Periods and the Multi-Site Depreciation Schedule

Medical equipment generally falls under 5- or 7-year MACRS recovery, 5-year for most diagnostic and treatment equipment, 7-year for general depreciable assets. Without §168(k), six locations with five-year assets would recover cost over the MACRS schedule using the half-year convention. With bonus depreciation, the full basis is expensed in year one.

For a detailed breakdown of recovery class assignments across equipment types, see [MACRS recovery periods for medical equipment](https://mddeductions.com/blog/medical-equipment-depreciation-schedule-macrs). The multi-site model doesn't change the classification rules, it multiplies the number of assets subject to them.

## Multi-Site Material Participation Documentation

### Distributing the 500-Hour Standard Across Locations

Under IRC §469 and Treas. Reg. §1.469-5T, an owner must log more than 500 hours in the activity annually to meet material participation, clearing the threshold that reclassifies the activity from passive to active and allows losses to offset W-2 income. For [IRS operating business requirements for medical devices](https://mddeductions.com/blog/medical-device-operating-business-requirements-irs), examiners look at whether the owner's involvement is genuine and recurring, not whether the owner can produce a single affidavit.

Six locations make this credible. If an owner spends an average of two hours per week per site on operations, staff oversight, protocol review, billing audit, vendor coordination, site visits, that's 12 hours per week across six sites. Annualized, that exceeds 600 hours without any single-site hour count looking manufactured.

Qualifying activities include:
- On-site visits and operational oversight
- Staff scheduling and performance review
- Clinical protocol management and vendor negotiations
- Billing and collections review
- Financial reporting and cash management decisions

Hour logs must be contemporaneous. A spreadsheet reconstructed at tax time under audit pressure is far less defensible than calendar entries, travel records, and meeting notes accumulated week by week. The six-site structure generates natural documentation events, because each site requires genuine, recurring attention.

Multi-site material participation documentation, done right, doesn't feel like a compliance exercise. It reflects what a real operator actually does.

## Staffing Across Locations as IRS Proof of Substance

Equipment and entity structure create the skeleton. Payroll creates the heartbeat.

Real nurses, infusion technicians, front-desk staff, and billing personnel employed across six sites is the clearest signal an IRS examiner looks for when evaluating whether a wellness business is a genuine operating enterprise. Geographic diversification of the workforce, not just equipment, is what separates this model from a passive or paper-only structure.

Tax practitioners who defend multi-location operating businesses before IRS examiners point to three markers: separate lease agreements, site-specific payroll records, and revenue traceable to each location. A six-location model generates all three by design. Each subsidiary produces its own payroll run, its own lease obligation, and its own revenue ledger.

The examiner's question is simple: does this business do what it claims to do? Six locations with six payrolls and six treatment schedules answers that question before it can be asked. The [cash-pay wellness equipment business model](https://mddeductions.com/blog/cash-pay-wellness-medical-equipment-business-model) reinforces why this structure is commercially viable across markets, revenue doesn't depend on insurance credentialing or network participation, which means each site can operate independently in its local market.

## Geographic Diversification and the Franchise vs. Owned Equipment Decision

Geographic diversification reduces single-market revenue risk and strengthens the business substance argument at the same time. A six-site operator drawing revenue from multiple markets demonstrates that the enterprise isn't a tax structure wearing a business's clothes, it's a business that also happens to have an optimized tax structure.

The franchise vs. owned equipment question is where many operators make a costly mistake.

Franchise arrangements that license equipment to an operator rather than vest title in the investor disqualify the investor from §168(k) bonus depreciation entirely. The statute requires the taxpayer to be the *owner* of the depreciable property placed in service, not a licensee. If the franchisor owns the equipment and licenses its use under the franchise agreement, the operator cannot claim the deduction, regardless of how the arrangement is marketed. See [bonus depreciation: equipment vs. real estate](https://mddeductions.com/blog/bonus-depreciation-real-estate-vs-equipment) for a direct comparison of how ownership title drives eligibility across asset classes.

Owning the equipment at each location, through the subsidiary or operating entity, preserves full §168(k) eligibility and keeps the deduction where it belongs: with the operator who placed the asset in service and runs the business around it. Across six locations, that distinction is the difference between a strategy that works and one that looks like it should work but fails on examination.
