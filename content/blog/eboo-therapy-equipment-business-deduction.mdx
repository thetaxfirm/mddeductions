If you own or are planning to acquire extracorporeal blood oxygenation equipment, the tax mechanics deserve as much attention as the clinical protocol. An EBOO therapy equipment business deduction is not a loophole, it's the straightforward application of §168(k) and §179 to a capital-intensive medical device placed in service inside a real operating business. Done right, the year-one deduction on a six-figure equipment purchase offsets meaningful active income. Done wrong, passive ownership, no staff, no patients, it's the kind of deduction that doesn't survive a letter from the IRS.

This is the playbook for doing it right.

## What EBOO Equipment Actually Costs, and Why It Qualifies

### Extracorporeal blood oxygenation equipment cost: the acquisition range

EBOO systems are purpose-built medical devices: a recirculating ozone generator, a specialized dialysis-style membrane, a blood circuit, and supporting IV infrastructure. Turnkey EBOO units from established vendors generally fall in the $30,000–$80,000 range per system, with fully outfitted treatment suites, including ancillary ozone equipment, monitoring hardware, and installation, reaching $100,000 or more per location. That acquisition range is where accelerated depreciation becomes material. A deduction in that range, claimed in Year 1, directly reduces taxable income by an amount most high-income professionals and practice owners notice.

### Asset classification and depreciation eligibility

IRS eligibility for accelerated depreciation requires the asset to be tangible depreciable property with a recovery period of 20 years or less. EBOO systems, classified as durable medical equipment, fall within the 5- to 7-year MACRS range under IRS Rev. Proc. 87-56 asset class guidelines and [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946). That recovery period makes them eligible for both §168(k) bonus depreciation and §179 expensing. The asset must be placed in service, operational, not just ordered or delivered, before the tax year closes.

## EBOO Equipment Depreciation Schedule: §168(k) vs. §179 Side by Side

### Bonus depreciation under §168(k) in the current tax year

Under §168(k) as enacted by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act and phased through current law, qualifying medical equipment placed in service in 2026 remains eligible for accelerated bonus depreciation. The applicable percentage has stepped down from 100% under the TCJA phase-down schedule, confirm the precise 2026 rate with your tax advisor, but even at a reduced rate, the year-one deduction on a $75,000 system is a meaningful front-loaded offset against active income.

The critical mechanical advantage of §168(k): it can generate a net operating loss. If your practice's ordinary income in 2026 is $200,000 and you place a $75,000 EBOO system in service, the §168(k) deduction reduces taxable income dollar-for-dollar. If the deduction exceeds the year's income, the resulting NOL carries forward under current rules. For more on how these rules apply across the medical device category, see the [§168(k) bonus depreciation rules for medical equipment](mddeductions.com/blog/section-168k-bonus-depreciation-medical-equipment).

### §179 expensing: limits and active-income guardrails

§179 also allows first-year expensing, but with a key structural difference: it cannot generate or increase a net loss. The deduction is capped at the entity's taxable income from active business operations. In 2026, the §179 deduction limit sits at $1,160,000 (adjusted for inflation), but that limit only matters if your business has sufficient active income to absorb it.

For an EBOO practice generating $150,000 in its first operating year, §179 can fully expense the equipment cost up to that income amount. Anything beyond that carries forward, deferred, not lost. §168(k) has no such income floor, which is why it's the more aggressive and often more valuable tool for high-income owners deploying capital in Year 1.

**The practical choice**: high-income owners with substantial outside active income typically maximize §168(k) first. §179 serves as a secondary lever or applies when §168(k) eligibility is partially phased. Run both scenarios with a tax advisor before placing the asset in service.

## Material Participation: The Requirement That Separates Active Deductions from Passive Losses

This is where most structures fail. Under IRC §469, losses and deductions from a business activity are only deductible against active income if the taxpayer materially participates in that activity. Without material participation, deductions become passive losses, usable only against passive income, not W-2 or business earnings.

The IRS defines material participation using seven tests. In practice, two are most relevant for practice owners operating an EBOO business:

- **500-hour test**: you participated in the activity for more than 500 hours during the tax year.
- **Substantially all test**: your participation constitutes substantially all participation in the activity by all individuals, including non-owners.

A high-income physician or entrepreneur cannot simply purchase EBOO equipment, hand it to a manager, and claim an active deduction. That arrangement is passive by definition. To claim the deduction against ordinary income, you need documented participation, calendars, logs, operational records, that supports one of the seven tests.

Tax practitioners working in the medical equipment space consistently find that §168(k) is most defensible when the operating entity has employees, generates documented revenue, and the owner meets at least one of the IRC §469 material participation tests. The deduction is real; the participation must be too.

## How to Acquire EBOO Technology for Your Practice

### Licensing, credentialing, and staffing requirements

EBOO involves extracorporeal blood processing, which triggers clinical oversight requirements that vary by state. In most jurisdictions, the procedure requires an RN or higher-level clinician to administer the protocol; some states require direct physician supervision or a standing physician order. Before acquiring equipment, confirm your state's specific requirements with a healthcare attorney.

Staff credentialing matters beyond compliance, it's the operational backbone of the business. A two-person clinical team (RN plus front-desk) can run a profitable single-system practice at moderate session volume. As volume scales, additional clinical staff are required; model that cost into your pro forma before running tax projections.

### Acquisition path: direct purchase vs. structured operating entity

The cleanest acquisition structure for an EBOO therapy equipment business deduction is a dedicated LLC or professional corporation (PC) that owns the equipment, employs clinical staff, and collects session revenue. The equipment depreciates on the entity's books; the deduction flows through to the owner's Schedule K-1 or personal return depending on entity type.

Key structural steps:

1. Form the operating entity (LLC or PC) in your state.
2. Open a dedicated business bank account before the first equipment payment.
3. Execute an equipment purchase agreement directly in the entity's name.
4. Hire or contract clinical staff and establish employment records prior to the first patient session.
5. Document the operational start date clearly, this is the "placed in service" date for depreciation purposes.
6. Engage a tax advisor to elect §168(k) and/or §179 on the entity's return for the year of acquisition.

Purchasing equipment personally and later transferring it to the entity complicates the depreciation timeline and introduces basis questions. Buy through the entity from day one.

## Ozone Therapy Equipment Tax Write-Off: Common Mistakes to Avoid

The ozone therapy equipment tax write-off fails for predictable reasons. Three mistakes account for the majority of IRS exposure:

**1. Passive ownership without material participation.** Buying the equipment, leasing it to a clinic you don't actively manage, and claiming the full deduction against W-2 income is not an active deduction, it's a passive loss dressed up as one. The structure collapses under §469 scrutiny.

**2. Paper transaction, no real operations.** The deduction must be grounded in a genuine business. An entity with no employees, no patients, and no revenue history, but a $75,000 equipment deduction on its return, is a red flag at any income level. Real operations mean real documentation: intake records, treatment logs, receipts, payroll records, state licensing filings.

**3. Misclassifying the asset and using the wrong recovery period.** EBOO equipment belongs in the 5- to 7-year MACRS class. Misclassifying it as 15-year property understates the available depreciation; treating ancillary fixtures as part of the medical device overstates the equipment basis. Each piece of capital expenditure in the clinic should be classified at the asset level, especially when EBOO and broader ozone therapy equipment share a single entity. Commingled assets with inconsistent recovery periods invite adjustment.

Avoid all three and the EBOO therapy equipment business deduction is straightforward, well-supported by current law, and defensible on audit.
