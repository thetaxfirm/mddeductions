## Plasmapheresis Equipment Cost and Depreciation Basis

### What Goes Into the Cost Basis

The depreciable basis for a plasmapheresis acquisition is broader than the sticker price on the apheresis machine. Under IRS capitalization rules, the following components roll into the basis:

- **The apheresis unit itself**, clinical-grade therapeutic plasmapheresis machines from manufacturers like Fresenius or Haemonetics carry significant per-unit capital costs
- **Installation and site preparation**, electrical upgrades, plumbing modifications, and integration with existing clinical infrastructure
- **Ancillary hardware**, IV poles, centrifuge accessories, monitoring equipment, and infusion pumps used in the protocol
- **Software and control systems**, if bundled with the equipment purchase, these are typically treated as part of the same depreciable asset
- **Freight and delivery**, costs to get the equipment to the placed-in-service location

All of these costs, when properly documented and capitalized, form the basis on which §168(k) and §179 elections operate. The higher the legitimate basis, the larger the potential year-one write-off.

### §168(k) Bonus Depreciation: The Year-One Write-Off Mechanics

§168(k) bonus depreciation lets you deduct a percentage of a qualified property's cost basis in the year the equipment is placed in service, rather than depreciating it over the standard [MACRS](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p946.pdf) recovery period. Plasmapheresis equipment qualifies as 5-year MACRS property, making it eligible for the bonus depreciation election.

The 100% rate that applied through 2022 has stepped down under the scheduled phaseout. Owners placing equipment in service in 2026 should confirm the current applicable percentage with a tax advisor, the rate has continued to decline annually, but the structural play remains compelling for high active-income earners because even a partial first-year write-off on a significant capital purchase produces a real tax offset.

§179 can be elected alongside or in place of §168(k), subject to its own income limitations and phase-out thresholds. In practice, the two elections are often layered to maximize year-one deductions. Both require that the equipment be placed in service in an active trade or business, not held passively.

## The Cash-Pay Plasmapheresis Business Model

### Why Cash-Pay Insulates Revenue from Reimbursement Risk

A cash-pay plasmapheresis practice collects payment directly from patients at the time of service. No insurance claims, no CPT code disputes, no remittance cycles stretching 60 to 90 days. Revenue is recognized when the session occurs. That predictability matters: you can model capacity utilization against fixed costs without building in adjudication uncertainty.

Cash-pay also eliminates prior authorization requirements, which delay treatment initiation and create patient attrition. When a patient commits to a protocol, say, six sessions over three months, they pre-pay or pay per session without insurance intermediaries. Margins reflect that directly.

### Plasmapheresis Therapy Revenue Potential

Revenue is driven by session volume, pricing tier, and protocol design. Cash-pay positioning in the concierge wellness and longevity medicine market allows per-session pricing at rates that would be heavily compressed under commercial insurance schedules. Practices pairing plasmapheresis with complementary modalities, such as EBOO (extracorporeal blood ozone oxygenation), increase per-visit yield by bundling protocols under a single patient visit.

Membership structures further stabilize revenue: patients commit to recurring monthly or quarterly sessions, generating predictable forward revenue rather than episodic one-time payments. This active, recurring cash-pay income is exactly what the §168(k) and §179 deductions are designed to offset in the acquisition year.

## Medical Equipment Business Deductions Beyond Depreciation

The year-one depreciation deduction is the headline, but the operating cost structure generates ongoing deductions across the full life of the business. Under [IRC §162](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/162), ordinary-and-necessary business expenses are deductible in the year incurred. For a plasmapheresis operation, that stack includes:

- **Staffing**, licensed nurses and phlebotomy technicians are a core operating expense; wages, benefits, and payroll taxes are fully deductible
- **Facility lease**, rent on a clinical suite, medspa, or dedicated wellness center is a §162 deduction in each period paid
- **Consumables**, apheresis tubing sets, anticoagulants, replacement bags, and disposable accessories are consumed per session and deducted as cost of goods or operating supplies
- **Maintenance contracts**, annual service agreements on the apheresis units protect both the equipment and the deduction basis, and are fully deductible as operating expenses
- **Professional fees**, legal, compliance, and administrative costs associated with running a licensed medical operation are ordinary-and-necessary expenses

This stack matters because it answers the assumption that the play is a single write-off. A real operating plasmapheresis business generates a multi-year deduction profile on top of the year-one equipment deduction.

## Active Income Requirements and Material Participation

The deduction only offsets active income if you materially participate in the business under [IRC §469](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/469). Passive activity losses, from businesses where the owner does not materially participate, cannot offset W-2 wages, practice income, or other active income. This is the compliance linchpin of the structure.

The IRS provides seven material participation tests; two are most relevant here:

1. **The 500-hour test**, the owner participates for more than 500 hours in the activity during the tax year
2. **The principal-activity test**, the owner's participation constitutes substantially all participation in the activity by all individuals (including non-owners) for the year

The most defensible position combines logged hours with documented operational involvement: hiring decisions, protocol oversight, vendor contracts, and compliance management. You don't need to perform every clinical function, but you need a documented, substantive role in running the business.

This is what distinguishes a real plasmapheresis business from a passive tax shelter. A paper structure with no staff, no patients, and no operating protocols will fail IRS scrutiny under §469 and potentially trigger accuracy-related penalties. Real staff, real locations, and real clinical protocols are not optional, they're what makes the deduction defensible.

## Acquiring Plasmapheresis Equipment: Tax Benefits and Operational Readiness

The acquisition decision has a timing dimension that directly affects the deduction. Equipment must be **placed in service**, operational and available for use in the business, before December 31 of the tax year in which you want to claim the write-off. Ordered but not installed equipment does not qualify. Operational readiness (staffing, licensure, facility lease, and compliance setup) needs to run concurrent with the equipment acquisition, not after it.

A complete acquisition plan includes:

- **Cost basis documentation**, invoices, freight receipts, installation contracts, and capitalized ancillary costs aggregated before closing the year
- **Placed-in-service confirmation**, a record demonstrating the equipment was operational by year-end
- **Material participation planning**, a documented role and hour log starting from the date the business is active
- **State licensure and compliance**, operating a therapeutic plasmapheresis practice requires appropriate clinical oversight; requirements vary by state but are a prerequisite for the business to be substantiated
